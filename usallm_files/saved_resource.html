W. E. Moerner — from trying to store 1,000 bits in one spot of light at IBM, to seeing a single molecule, to watching biology at 10 nanometres.

The trick was never to beat diffraction head-on. It was to turn molecules on and off, one at a time.

Chapter 1 · In order

The wall

Every microscope hits the same wall. Light spreads — so two things that are close together stop looking like two things.

The rule

The spot a point of light makes is about λ / 2 — roughly 200–250 nm across. That spot is the point-spread function, or PSF.

Why it matters

Biology lives below that size. Two close proteins merge into one blur. You cannot sharpen your way out of it by buying a better camera.

“You cannot tell them apart — not because the camera is bad, but because light spreads.”

Chapter 2 · In order

Smaller source, better picture

If a smaller source makes a better picture, why not make the light source itself tiny — and scan it across the surface?

The lesson Moerner carries forward: ask every method for its pros and its cons. Near-field is elegant — and limited to the surface. That honesty becomes the habit for the whole field.

✓ Pro: smaller source✕ Con: surface onlyRule: ask for both

Chapter 3 · In order

From bits to molecules

Mid-1980s, IBM Almaden. The goal wasn’t biology at all. It was optical storage — packing more bits into light.

How hole-burning worked

Burn a mark at one colour. Burn another at a different colour. Read them back by scanning the laser: 1 – 0 – 1 – 0.

What survived

Extreme sensitivity. FM spectroscopy. Tunable dye lasers. And a habit of caring, obsessively, about signal-to-noise.

Chapter 4 · In order

The √N leap to one

Managers said: do the most exciting science you can think of. Moerner did a piece of arithmetic — and it changed what “impossible” meant.

In 1989, with a postdoc, using FM spectroscopy, Moerner detected a single molecule optically: pentacene in p-terphenyl at low temperature. People thought it was impossible — “too small.” The √N argument said otherwise.

5
Chapter 5 · In order

How do you know it’s one?

Seeing a spot isn’t proof. You have to make the crowd thin out until only one voice is left — and then check it behaves like one.

First filter

Dilute, and go to the wings of the inhomogeneous line where molecules are sparse. See rare, isolated spots.

Later proofs

Antibunching / photon correlation — one emitter can’t emit two photons at once. And single-step on/off.

Chapter 6 · In order

Room temperature + blinking

At room temperature the neat frequency trick disappears. All absorptions broaden. So you dilute far more — and then a surprise blinks at you.

1997 surprise: single GFP blinks. Postdoc Patrick Ambrose had seen molecules “jumping around” — low-temperature spectral jumps. Rob Dixon in San Diego saw on/off. Critics said blinking was wasting time.

“Blinking is physics to understand — and it became the key.”

Blinking = signal, not noiseCritics: wasting timeMoerner: understand it

Chapter 7 · In order

Localization is not yet resolution

One molecule alone gives you super-localization — not super-resolution. That distinction is the whole game.

Fit the blob. Find its centre. Precision improves as 1 / √Nphotons. With enough photons, you can locate one molecule to about 10 nm — but you still haven’t resolved two molecules that are on at the same time.

8
Chapter 8 · In order

Turn them on/off — PALM, STORM, PAINT

To get true super-resolution, separate overlapping PSFs in time. Only one ON per spot per frame. Localize it. Repeat thousands of times. Build a pointillist image.

PALM / Betzig

Molecules dark until blue light switches one ON. Measure it. Then the next.

STORM

Chemistry controls blinking. Dial the conditions so only a few are ON at once.

PAINT / DNA-PAINT

Dim molecules in solution get bright only when they bind, then unbind. Origin: Hochstrasser’s membrane-binding dye.

Chapter 9 · In order

The other horse — STED donut + SIM

There was never one road. While some labs turned molecules on and off in time, others reshaped the light itself.

Structured illumination (SIM) — Mats Gustafsson: fringe patterns, moved and rotated, give 2× below the limit; nonlinear SIM goes further.

STED — Stefan Hell: the donut is made by a spiral phase plate / corkscrew. Crank up the donut power and the dark centre squeezes narrower. Early STED was only 2× — then better donuts, a darker centre, and less aberration made it better and better.

Chapter 10 · In order

What we suddenly saw

Get 5–50× better, and you have to re-do all the old imaging. Suddenly, structures that were invisible were just… there.

Shuang lab · axons

Proteins form rings spaced ~100–190 nm along the axon. Invisible before.

Lucy Shapiro · bacteria

Not bags of enzymes. Internal structures for division.

SARS-CoV-2 infected cell

Around the dark nucleus, clusters = replication organelles. 100s–1,000 copies of viral RNA per cluster, from one virus.

Chapter 11 · In order

Watching one move

For fast dynamics, don’t build a super-resolution image. Dilute further. Watch ONE molecule.

Measure on/off times and you measure kinetics directly. The same single-molecule idea watches molecular motors stepping and chromosomes moving and binding in the nucleus.

Chapter 12 · In order

Going 3D + orientation

A normal microscope is 2D — XY. Biology isn’t. So change what the PSF looks like, and let its shape tell you Z.

Orientation: a molecule is a dipole emitter. Split the polarizations and read the phase patterns, and you learn which way it points. Anecdote: Yoav Shechtman once gave Moerner a picture where a phase pattern on one side makes his face from a single emitter.

Chapter 13 · In order

The price: labels

Fluorescence needs a label. That is the price of all this beauty — and it has to be paid carefully.

Label carefully

Attach a dye/antibody to the protein of interest, or a fluorescent protein 3–5 nm across. It must not perturb — so run controls, try small vs. large labels, and check behaviour.

Label-free

Interferometric scattering (iSCAT, Michelle Kuipers) — about 120 nm now, and promising. Plus scattering / photothermal single molecules as hero experiments.

14
Chapter 14 · In order

Every method has pros and cons

Back to the rule from Section 2. No method wins everything. The honest question is always: what does it buy you, and what does it cost?

Single-molecule method: relatively simple, many labs can do it, ~10 nm easily — but it takes time to build up positions. Push blinking faster: York Beurskens at video rates.

Why fluorescence exploded: single-molecule sensitivity is easy. New directions: nanoparticles that blink, spectral information that tells local environment, ODMR spin work — 1993 pentacene triplet, RF + microwaves → magnetic sensing, now NV centres and GFP ODMR. And bowtie gold nano-antennas altering emission.

Chapter 15 · In order

The dream

All at once. That’s the dream — and it is deliberately unreasonable.

“If it had been very difficult for every single experiment, it wouldn’t have exploded nearly as much as it has.”

Moerner’s take: the field keeps growing because millions of brilliant people keep throwing ideas together. And the advice that started it all still holds:

“Do the most exciting science you can think of.”

Moerner’s rules

Four habits to carry away

  1. Ask pros / cons — of every method.
  2. Blinking is signal, not noise.
  3. √N thinking — check the scaling before you call it impossible.
  4. Label carefully — and prove you didn’t perturb it.
