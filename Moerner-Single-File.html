Section 01

The square-root shortcut

The recording opens in the middle of an argument — and the argument is what made single molecules seem possible at all.

A lot of people thought it was not possible to see single molecules. Moerner’s managers had told him: don’t get too focused on the application, just do the most exciting science you can think of. So he asked: why not try to see a single molecule?

The encouragement was arithmetic. Suppose you have a thousand molecules in resonance. The size of the feature is the square root of a thousand — about 30. Once you have measured something 30 big, you realise it is only the square root of n harder to get to n = 1. A 30-times-better experiment is easier than a 1,000-times-better one.

That’s what encouraged us to try to go to the single molecule limit.

Moerner, on the square-root argument

One law, two appearances

The square root returns later: the more photons you collect from one blinking molecule, the better you know where it is — improving as 1/√N. The same statistics that made the first experiment seem possible also set the precision of every super-resolution image.

Section 02

A horse race beneath the diffraction limit

Asked for an overview, Moerner starts with the physics everyone was fighting.

Light does not focus to a point. Every spot blurs to roughly half a wavelength — λ/2, a few hundred nanometres in the visible. Nothing in the image is smaller than that, whatever the sample contains.

One way around it is to make the source smaller rather than the lens better. That was near-field optics: a fibre drawn to a sharp tip, coated in aluminium, light leaking from a tiny hole, scanned across the surface. Eric Betzig worked on it, as did groups in Switzerland. Moerner calls it “very nice” — and notes it works right on the surface.

Near-field

Shrink the source. Works on the surface.

Structured illumination (SIM)

Change the illumination. Pattern + computation.

STED

Switch molecules off everywhere except a point.

Localisation (PALM / STORM)

Let molecules light up one at a time, locate each.

Almost all methods have some positives and some negatives. They have advantages and disadvantages. It is always important to make sure people tell you what the advantages and disadvantages of their technique are.

Moerner, on method-talk
Section 03

The donut that switches molecules off

STED, rebuilt from the ground up — including the part that sounds impossible: throwing photons away.

The donut approach is Stefan Hell’s stimulated emission depletion microscopy. Start with an ordinary confocal microscope and add one ingredient: a ring-shaped beam, bright all around and dark in the middle, laid over the same focus. Molecules in the bright ring are forced to emit immediately and are therefore never available to fluoresce for the camera. What is left is the narrow region at the centre where the ring is dark.

How do you remove the fluorescence photons? The depletion light drives stimulated emission at a different wavelength from spontaneous fluorescence, so a filter can remove the depletion light, while the depletion itself has already silenced the molecules in the ring.

You are depleting their fluorescence because you are forcing them to emit … and you are throwing those photons away. Filters. You filter out the stimulated light.

The exchange over STED, condensed

Then the sharpest question: is resolution not simply limited by the size of the hole in the donut? Diffraction does apply to the donut — the ring itself is diffraction-limited. But the very centre can be essentially zero intensity. Turn the total power up, and the ring gets brighter while the centre stays dark. The region that is not depleted narrows. Nothing about the lens changed; the geometry of the light did the work. His interviewers call it using diffraction against itself.

It was not instant. Getting a factor of two below the limit was exciting. Getting better and better took better donuts, a darker centre, less aberration. If light leaks into the centre, it kills the effect. Improvement after improvement of every optical component around the idea.

Remember, I am not a STED expert. We built our own STED microscope and used it for a certain number of experiments, but the real experts are those who worked in that field for twenty, thirty, forty years.

Moerner, on borrowing a technique
Section 04

How to make a donut

A spiral of phase, a corkscrew of light, and a mode of the electromagnetic field.

How do you make a donut? Several ways to think about it. One is to have light pass through a spiral of phase — an optical component whose thickness changes steadily around a full turn. Send light through that and it twists like a corkscrew. You cannot have electric field at the centre of it. So it makes a donut.

Another way: it is one of the solutions of the electromagnetic field. TEM00 is a Gaussian. TEM11 will be a donut. Just another mode, which you want to use.

Moerner mentions several ways rather than a single recipe, and moves on quickly — the donut is not his instrument. The point he keeps returning to is that precision here comes from light intensity, which can be tuned continuously, rather than from any machined feature, which cannot.

Section 05

From crystals to cells

The same person, the same question, a different sample.

Moerner began not in biology but in optical data storage and spectroscopy in crystals and polymers. The question was always signal and noise: how do you see something very small against a background?

When the single-molecule idea moved into cells, the stakes changed. A crystal is tidy. A cell is crowded, moving, autofluorescent, and full of things you did not label. The physics did not change, but the sample did. That is why the later sections keep returning to labels, background, and whether a picture can be believed.

~250 nmordinary spot, visible light
~10 nmsuper-resolution, in good cases
5–50×better than diffraction-limited
Section 06

Frequency as a dimension

Before single molecules, there was spectral hole-burning.

In a crystal or glass at low temperature, absorption is inhomogeneously broadened — not one line but a dense pile of very narrow peaks, one for each molecule or defect, each at a slightly different frequency because each sits in a slightly different local environment.

That spread makes frequency usable as a dimension. Instead of one bit per spot, you could write hundreds to a thousand bits in the same spot, choosing the colour at which each is written and reading them back by tuning the laser. One host offers the analogy of a disc written and read with light. Moerner takes it further: same idea, far smaller scale, many more bits per spot.

The idea dates from the mid-1970s, pursued at IBM by spectroscopists trying it in every host they could think of. Moerner worked mostly on the molecular side. His slice was not the writing mechanism but the signal-to-noise: how deep a mark can you burn, how strong is the signal, what does the background look like?

Section 07

Why fluorescence wins

Absorption is a whisper. Fluorescence is a shout on a dark background.

Early work measured absorption — a tiny dip in a bright beam. Hard. Fluorescence changed the game: if you can get rid of background, if you can have very low background everywhere, then fluorescence is on a dark background.

If you can get rid of background, if you can have very low background everywhere, then fluorescence is on a dark background.

Moerner, on the decisive change

That is why a single molecule, which absorbs almost nothing, can still be seen: it can emit many photons, one after another, against darkness. The rest of the story is about collecting those photons efficiently and not being fooled by them.

Section 08

First single molecule

1989, in a crystal, at low temperature — and then the long road to room temperature and cells.

The first single-molecule optical detection used absorption in a crystal, with frequency selection to pick one molecule out of the pile. It proved the square-root argument was not just arithmetic.

Then came fluorescence, then room temperature, then single molecules in polymers, then in cells. Each step added background, motion, and chemistry. Each step also made the result more biological — and more worth believing only if the controls were right.

Section 09

Saturation and the narrowest line

Sitting on one molecule, turning the power up, and finding a width limited by nothing but lifetime.

One thing you can do with a single molecule, never with an ensemble, is follow it as a function of power. Sit on the molecule, tune across it to measure its line width, then sit on the peak and increase laser power, watching signal climb and then stop climbing — saturate. Standard three-level models let you calculate the shape of that curve and the intensity at which the knee occurs. Moerner calls the exercise fascinating, partly because it worked so well.

Section 10

The triplet — nuisance, then tool

A dark state that ruins an experiment, then returns as a measurement.

Molecules occasionally make an intersystem crossing into a long-lived triplet state, where they go dark and stay dark. For early experiments you deliberately chose molecules with a weak triplet yield — molecules that would not disappear on you, but would come back and absorb again and again.

He flags, without developing it yet, that the triplet will return later for entirely different reasons: those spin levels turn out to be usable. The nuisance becomes a probe. Keep that in mind for Section 23.

Section 11

Blinking, 1997

A green fluorescent protein that blinks — and blinking becomes the method.

In 1997, a green fluorescent protein was found to blink: on, off, on again, without being told to. At first that looks like a problem. Then it looks like the method. If molecules blink, you can separate them in time even when you cannot separate them in space.

That is the key turn from “see one molecule” to “image many molecules at super-resolution”: do not try to see them all at once. See them one at a time.

Section 12

Controlling the blink

From accidental blinking to photoactivation, photoswitching, and DNA-PAINT.

Once blinking is the idea, you want control. Photoactivatable proteins that turn on with one colour and are read with another. Photoswitchable dyes that can be pushed on and off. And later, DNA-PAINT: a target strand anchored to the structure, a short imager strand with a light on it that docks, glows, and lets go — blinking by binding, not by chemistry inside the dye.

Even if they are at different random positions, you can measure all their positions. That is the key idea for super resolution with single molecules.

Moerner, on photoactivation
Section 13

Localisation is not resolution

A precise position is not the same as a resolved image.

Moerner is careful here. Super-localisation — knowing where one isolated emitter is, very precisely — is not by itself super-resolution. Resolution is about two objects, close together, that you can tell apart. Localisation is the tool. The image is built by repeating it thousands of times and drawing all the positions.

Distinction to keep

Localisation: where is this one molecule? Resolution: can I tell these two apart? The second needs the first, plus density, labelling, and controls.

Section 14

Where precision comes from

Photons are the currency.

How precisely can you locate one molecule? Roughly, the width of its blur divided by the square root of the number of photons you collect — plus background, pixel size, and drift. More photons, better precision. That is why brightness, low background, and not bleaching too fast matter so much.

There is also a newer family of ideas: put a donut beam right on top of the molecule, so it emits nothing at all. Move a tiny distance and you suddenly get good information. Position becomes something you infer from a null. Moerner calls the whole scheme a way to determine position more precisely with fewer photons — which, since photons are the currency, is the only kind of improvement that matters.

And a caution: through all of this you are sensing the average position of an electric dipole — the emitter announcing itself by its oscillating dipole field. Nothing here tells you about individual atoms inside the molecule. He returns to this later, when someone asks what 0.1 nm precision really signifies. The answer — the average position of the whole object — is set up here.

Section 15

What was actually new

Five, ten, fifty times better is not incremental — it is a different dataset.

Was there a hero experiment, something that startled people outside the single-molecule community? Moerner gives both halves. First, quantitative: if you can measure with resolution five, ten or fifty times better than diffraction-limited — down to ~10 nm rather than 100–200 nm — then you have dramatically more information.

Second, qualitative: sometimes that reveals a different phenomenon. Examples: a cross-section of an axon with a labelled protein forming rings spaced ~100 nm apart — invisible to ordinary optics. A molecular motor walking along its track, each step measurable, because a single emitter can be followed as it moves. Work with Lucy Shapiro on proteins inside bacteria: bacteria are not simply bags of enzymes; they contain internal structures that matter for how they divide, and those structures had been invisible.

Then the class of results that is not about resolution at all. If you want to watch something really fast, conventional super-resolution can be the wrong tool, because building up locations takes time and you do not want molecules moving while you build. So you drop to the single-molecule limit and follow one thing, fast.

Section 16

One virus, a thousand copies

Super-resolution applied to something that is very much not a toy sample.

A cell infected by SARS-CoV-2, mapped with the blinking method. Replication organelles cluster into little clusters around a dark nucleus — no label gets in there. The image was made with the ordinary super-resolution workflow, applied to a real infection.

Section 17

The label problem

You never see the protein. You see the label.

Every fluorescence image is an image of labels, not of the thing itself. If the label is big, if it binds in the wrong place, if it does not get in, if it blinks badly — the picture lies, politely. That is why controls, labelling density, and knowing what your label does matter more than the microscope.

Rule

Ask of every super-resolution picture: what is labelled, how densely, and what is missing? If the answer is not in the paper, the picture is not yet believable.

Section 18

Two microscopes, one sample

Correlative imaging: light tells you where, electrons tell you what.

One way to check a fluorescence picture is to look at the same sample with another microscope — for example electron microscopy — and ask whether the structures agree. Light gives you identity (this protein, labelled). Electrons give you context (membranes, organelles, ultrastructure). Together they are more believable than either alone.

It is slow, fiddly, and worth it when the claim is strong.

Section 19

Making the emitter say more

Position is not the only thing a photon can tell you.

A single emitter can report more than where it is: its orientation (dipole direction), its spectrum, its lifetime, whether it is moving, whether it is bound. Moerner’s group has pushed this — measuring orientation and other properties in parallel with position, so one molecule says more per photon.

That matters because photons are scarce. If each one can carry two kinds of information instead of one, you win twice.

Section 20

The whole cell at once

From a thin slice to a volume.

Cells are three-dimensional. Imaging a thin plane is not enough if the biology lives in volume. Whole-cell approaches — light-sheet, clever illumination, and 3D localisation — try to get the whole cell at once, without bleaching it to death on the way.

The trade-off is always the same: speed vs. precision vs. photodamage. You cannot maximise all three.

Section 21

Is fluorescence the future?

A direct question, a careful answer.

Is fluorescence the future? Moerner does not claim it is the only future. Fluorescence is powerful because it is specific — you label what you want — and because it works in living cells. But it is not the only way to beat the diffraction limit, and it is not the only way to see small things. Electron microscopy, label-free methods, and new physics all have their place.

The future, in his telling, is not one technique winning. It is many techniques, each honest about its pros and cons, used where they fit.

Section 22

The third dimension

Depth encoded in an angle.

How do you get z? One elegant trick: make the spot into two spots that rotate around each other as the sample moves up and down. Depth is encoded in an angle. Look at the rotation, read the depth.

Section 23

Spins and the second life of the triplet

The nuisance from Section 10 returns — as a quantum sensor.

Those triplet spin levels, once a nuisance that made molecules go dark, turn out to be usable. Optically detected magnetic resonance (ODMR) and related ideas use light to read spin, and spin to sense magnetic fields, temperature, and local environment at the nanoscale.

It is a different kind of single-molecule measurement: not where is it, but what is the field where it sits? The same molecule, a new question.

Section 24

How far can it go?

0.1 nm precision — of what?

Someone asks what a precision of a tenth of a nanometre really signifies. Moerner’s answer returns to Section 14: you are sensing the average position of a dipole, of the whole object. Not individual atoms inside the molecule. Precision is not the same as seeing atoms.

Do not over-read precision

0.1 nm is a statement about the average position of an emitter, under good conditions, with many photons. It is not a photograph of atoms.

Section 25

What he tells people starting out

Advice, not technique.

Work on something you care about. Learn the fundamentals deeply enough that you can tell when an instrument is lying to you. Be honest about pros and cons — yours and others’. And remember that progress is often improvement after improvement of every component around a good idea, not one magic breakthrough.

Section 26

Sputnik kid

The interview ends where his curiosity began.

Moerner grew up in San Antonio, in the Sputnik era. He studied Russian for four years in high school — because at the time it seemed a good language to learn. Others were learning German and Italian for the same reason, and of course Spanish, growing up in San Antonio, was essential.

The closing thought is a worry, and it is the one place where the interview turns wistful. The essential thing, he says, is caring about how things work — and he is concerned that we have less of that now. We all carry around remarkable computers and many people have no idea what is inside them, even though there is an enormous amount of science in there. It is not too complex; you can learn about every part of it.

The obstacle is practical: components have become very small, you can no longer see the wires, and unsoldering something to see how it works has got harder. He allows that you can now buy a quite good little USB microscope, but points out that even that will not let you get inside a chip. You can still go to school and learn about every one of those components, he adds, and that is a pretty exciting thing.
