1Where abstraction is grounded

The excerpt opens with an admission about the way we think.

We humans operate on such a high degree of abstraction most of the time that it is very hard for us to wrap our mind around where it is grounded.

That is the question the passage sets out to work on. What are the actual physical experiences that have led to our highly abstract understanding of the world?

2You cannot think it without the words

The example chosen is climate change.

You cannot think of climate change without having the words for climate and change. The language is not decoration laid over an experience; it is part of what makes the thought available at all.

3What you can actually experience

Against that, the passage sets the things you can feel for yourself. You can experience temperature. You can experience humidity. You can experience that today is different from yesterday.

temperaturehumiditytoday different from yesterday

These are the raw materials. Everything abstract is said to be built out of them, at a distance.

4Detaching, really far away

Then comes the turn. To understand the concept of climate changing over hundreds of years, you need to detach yourself from those experiences — not a little, but really, really, really far away.

“You need to detach yourself from those experiences really, really, really far away and to compress them into these highly abstract units of language.”

From the middle of the source

The distance is the point. The concept of climate is not a bigger version of the feeling of warmth; it is the feeling of warmth with almost all of its particularity stripped off.

5Compressed into units of language

Two operations are named in that one line. The first is detachment: stepping back from the sensation. The second is compression: packing what is left into highly abstract units of language, so that a whole history of weather fits inside two words.

Drawn from the source: each rung keeps the pattern and drops the sensation it came from.

6Walking the route backwards

So it can be really difficult to go back and say, well, okay: where did this come from? The passage then does exactly that, in public, tracing the route from the concept back to the skin.

The route, in the order the source lists it.

7Neurons that pattern the information

The sequence named is this. The idea of climate comes from our skin experiencing warmth and our skin experiencing coldness. Then neurons pattern that information into concepts of cold, and then of hot. Then that is connected to the idea of ambient air.

Notice what is doing the compressing. It is not a word and not a person; it is a population of cells responding to a pattern in what just happened.

8So far away it is hard to say what it boils down to

Having traced the whole route, the passage lands on the difficulty rather than resolving it. It is just so far away from direct experience that it is really hard to say what it boils down to.

The question the source keeps returning to is not whether abstraction is real, but whether the thing it boils down to can still be named as a physical experience.

9Why sea slugs are easier to read

At this point the passage changes scale on purpose. Working with sea slugs was really revelatory, because sea slugs are a lot simpler — and it is easier to understand how they process their patterns of experience.

What a simpler animal gives you

  • A smaller set of parts to watch
  • Patterns of experience that can be followed directly
  • One clear example: what danger is to them

What the passage asks next

  • Not what danger feels like
  • What danger is, in the wiring
  • And whether the same answer scales up

10What danger means to a sea slug

Danger, to them, is any signal coming from any pain receptor anywhere in the body. No location is privileged and no single receptor is special. Any of them, anywhere, contributes the same kind of message.

11One neuron collects the signals

There is a neuron that collects all those signals from those different parts of the body. In the diagram, the many sources on the left are the many places the body can be hurt; the single cell on the right is where they meet.

Convergence, in the source’s own sequence.

12When it fires, that is the idea of danger

And when that neuron fires, the firing of that neuron represents the idea of danger. It is as simple as that.

The firing is not a label attached to the idea. On this account, the firing is the idea — the state of one cell standing for danger, because everything that matters has already been gathered into it.

13There is no further switch

One clarification follows immediately, because it blocks the obvious objection. There is no further switch somewhere that says: danger. Danger is just the fact that all of those different pain signals have converged in one point.

The temptation, and the correctionIt feels as though something must read the convergence and then announce it. The source says the convergence is already the whole event.

14The brain does this in a more complicated way

From the sea slug the passage turns back to us. The brain does it, the source says, in a more complicated way — but that is also true for how we process, understand and access these abstract features of reality.

So the simple case is offered not as a curiosity but as a template: the same shape of explanation, at a larger scale.

15No special climate-change unit

The negative claim comes next, and it is the heart of the passage. It is not that there is some special climate change in us, a thing that clicks in our brain when we perceive this concept.

What makes the concept available is the organization of the brain: it is a consequence of having our brain arranged in hierarchical levels, and of those levels processing our everyday experience.

16One neuron for heat, one for cold

The arrangement is spelled out in terms of who responds to what. One neuron responds to a pattern of heat. One neuron responds to a pattern of cold.

1
A pattern of heat, a pattern of cold. Separate cells answer separate patterns in what just happened.
2
Those cells join larger patterns. They form part of a greater pattern of cold weather and hot weather.
3
Then a greater pattern still. They form part of an even greater pattern of changes in cold weather and changes in hot weather.
4
And eventually the immense concept. By virtue of that hierarchical organization, we get to concepts like climate change.

17Patterns of patterns

Read one level at a time, nothing exotic is required at any step. A cell answers a pattern. The cells together form a pattern. Those patterns form a pattern of their own.

The hierarchy as the source describes it, bottom tier upward.

18Where does it sit? Nowhere

This is why the whole matter is hard to wrap your mind around. The question we want to ask is: okay, where in the brain is this? Where does this concept of climate change sit?

Point to that place in the brain and say, this is climate change — but you cannot. There is no place.

A concept with no address: what is left is a combination, not a location.

19All of it at once, influencing each other

The alternative is stated plainly. It is all the combination of all of the things that are related to climate change, activated and influencing each other at the same time, that forms this concept in our brain.

Combination is doing the work in that sentence twice over: the concept is the combination, and it is only there while the parts are active together.
