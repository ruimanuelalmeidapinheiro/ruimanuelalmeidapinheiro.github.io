Seeing Biology at Sub-Wavelength Scale — W.E. Moerner on Super-Resolution Microscopy

A single-molecule biography: from a spectral hole in a crystal to a living cell, told in the order the story was told.

Nobody believed one molecule could be seen. Moerner spends this conversation explaining why it can, why it matters, and what the picture actually means — a steady climb from the square root of a thousand down to the average position of a dipole.

Section 01

The square-root shortcut

The recording opens in the middle of an argument — before any optics, before any microscope — with a piece of statistics that made one molecule look reachable.

A lot of people thought it was not possible to see a single molecule, Moerner begins, and the reason they thought so is worth keeping in mind for the whole conversation. The objection was never that molecules are invisible in principle. It was that a single molecule is absurdly small — a few nanometres — while every optical instrument smears whatever it looks at into a blur a hundred times larger. You would be searching for one faint thing inside the noise of everything else.

His way past that objection was not a cleverer lens. It was an argument about how the signal grows. Suppose a thousand molecules sit at the same optical frequency, in resonance with the laser. The feature they produce together does not grow a thousand times bigger than the feature made by one molecule — it grows only as the square root of the count. That is about thirty. So the measured feature from a thousand molecules is roughly thirty times larger than the feature from one.

Turn that around and the whole problem changes shape:

But once you have measured this thing that is thirty big, you realise, wait a minute, it is only the square root of n harder to get to n equal to one. Thirty times better experiment is easier than a thousand times better experiment.

Moerner, in the cold open

Thirty times better is a large but ordinary improvement in an experiment. A thousand times better is a different project. That gap — the difference between a hard year and an impossible decade — is what encouraged him and a postdoc, in 1989, to try to reach the single-molecule limit. The rest of the interview is, in a sense, a long explanation of what happened once they got there.

One law, two appearances

The square root reappears later in the conversation in a completely different role: the more photons you collect from one blinking molecule, the better you know where it is — improving as one over the square root of the photon count. The same statistics that made the first experiment seem possible also sets the precision of every super-resolution image built from it.

Section 02

A horse race beneath the diffraction limit

Asked for an overview of the competing methods, Moerner starts with the physics everyone was fighting, then walks through the four or five families of tricks.

The first question of the interview asks him to lay out the horse race: which methods existed for getting below the diffraction limit, which ones ended up inside the Nobel Prize, and how the field evolved. He answers by reminding the audience of the underlying rule. Light does not focus to a point. Every spot blurs out to roughly half a wavelength — he puts it at lambda over two, which in the visible ranges around a few hundred nanometres. Nothing in the image is smaller than that, whatever the sample contains.

One way around it is to make the source smaller rather than the lens better. This was the field of near-field optics: an optical fibre drawn down to a very sharp tip, coated in aluminium, with light leaking out of a tiny hole at the end, scanned across the surface. Eric Betzig worked on this, as did groups in Switzerland, pushing the resolution higher and higher. Moerner calls the method "very nice" and notes its defining property: it works right on the surface.

A second family changes the illumination instead of the optics. Rather than flooding the sample with a uniform beam, you illuminate it with a pattern — most usefully with interference fringes of a known spacing and a known angle. This is structured illumination microscopy, which Moerner credits to Mats Gustafsson, who devised it before his early death. Mixing an unknown fine pattern with a known coarse one produces a moiré beat that can be measured, and by moving and rotating the fringes you can recover detail finer than the diffraction limit allows. On its own it buys a factor of two, which Moerner insists is a huge effect and not a ten-per-cent effect; made nonlinear, it goes further.

A third family keeps the optics and attacks the molecules, turning them off in a shaped way. That is Stefan Hell's stimulated emission depletion, discussed in the next section. And a fourth family keeps the molecules and separates them in time instead of in space: the single-molecule route that Moerner himself took, and the one the last decades of biology have leaned on hardest.

Before any of that, though, he adds a caution that applies to the entire menu:

Almost all methods have some positives and some negatives. They have advantages and disadvantages. It is always important to make sure people tell you what the advantages and disadvantages of their technique are.

Moerner, on method-talk
Section 03

The donut that switches molecules off

Stimulated emission depletion, rebuilt from the ground up in the conversation — including the part that sounds impossible: throwing photons away.

The donut approach, in Moerner's telling, is Stefan Hell's stimulated emission depletion microscopy. It starts from an ordinary confocal microscope and adds one ingredient: a ring-shaped beam of light, bright all around and dark in the middle, laid over the same focus. The molecules inside the bright ring are forced to emit their photons immediately and are therefore never available to fluoresce for the camera. What is left is the narrow region at the very centre of the focus where the ring is dark — and only there are molecules still free to answer.

The interview spends several turns on the obvious difficulty. Photons of fluorescence are the signal; how do you remove them? The answer is that the depletion light drives stimulated emission at a different wavelength from the spontaneous fluorescence, so a filter can remove the depletion light, while the depletion itself has already silenced the molecules in the ring.

You are depleting their fluorescence because you are forcing them to emit … and you are throwing those photons away. Filters. You filter out the stimulated light.

The exchange over STED, condensed

Then comes the sharpest question of the section, and the one with the most satisfying answer. Is the resolution not simply limited by the size of the hole in the middle of the donut? Moerner's reply is that diffraction does apply to the donut — the ring itself is diffraction-limited, with the usual pattern of bright, dim, bright. But the very centre can be essentially zero intensity. Turn the total power up, and the ring gets brighter and brighter while the centre stays dark. The region that is not being depleted narrows. Nothing about the lens changed; the geometry of the light did the work. His interviewers call it using diffraction against itself — squeezing the dark spot by making the bright parts brighter.

He is careful about the credit. Newer work by Hell, he says, uses the small dark spot itself in a way that Hell argues is not subject to the diffraction limit at all — a different point of view, which Hell must explain himself. Asked how STED progressed from being barely effective to the powerhouse it became, Moerner lists the unglamorous answer: better and better donuts, a darker centre, less aberration, less light leaking into the middle, improvement after improvement of every optical component around the idea. And he adds a candid disclaimer.

Remember, I am not a STED expert. We built our own STED microscope and used it for a certain number of experiments, but the real experts are those who worked in that field for twenty, thirty, forty years.

Moerner, on borrowing a technique
Section 04

How to make a donut

A corkscrew of phase, a mode of the electromagnetic field, and an axis where light is simply not allowed to exist.

Invited to explain to the audience how a donut beam is actually built, Moerner gives two descriptions. The first is mechanical: pass the light through a component carrying a spiral of phase — a glass surface whose thickness increases steadily all the way around, so that the wavefront comes out twisted, like a corkscrew. Send light through that and the centre of the beam turns out to be forbidden: you cannot have electric field along that axis, so what emerges is a ring. The second description is mathematical: the donut is simply another solution of the electromagnetic field, one mode among the many, and the game is to build optics that select it.

The practical consequence is what makes the trick robust. Because the dark core is a property of the mode rather than of any single lens or alignment, it survives the trip through the rest of the microscope; the ring can be made brighter and brighter without the middle filling in, which is exactly what the previous section needed. Later in the interview a related phase pattern comes back in a very different role — pointed not at the sample but at the shape of the image itself — and it is the same idea of remixing the modes of the light.

Moerner mentions several ways to think about it rather than a single recipe, and moves on quickly — the donut is not his instrument. The point he keeps returning to is that this piece of physics, once available, made an entire family of experiments possible, and that its precision comes from light intensity, which can be tuned continuously, rather than from any machined feature, which cannot.

Section 05

What the single-molecule route is good for

Asked to be honest about the pros and cons of his own method, Moerner gives both sides at once.

The advantages are the reason his approach spread to groups all over the world. It is relatively simple, a lot of people can do it, and ten-nanometre resolution comes fairly easily. It also has a special case that needs no label at all: some structures are already fluorescent. He gestures, in passing, at the trees around them, whose antenna proteins fluoresce natively, and says they can be studied through that native fluorescence without attaching anything.

The disadvantages are equally concrete. To be general, you still have to label whatever you want to see. And because a super-resolution picture is assembled from molecules blinking one at a time, it takes time to accumulate all those positions — so the method is slow by default. Groups have attacked that directly by making the blinking itself faster; one worker has pushed the technique to video frame rates. But the speed problem reasserts itself whenever you ask for very high resolution on very short timescales, which is to ask for everything at once.

There is a third cost that has nothing to do with hardware. When you build an image out of thousands of estimated positions, every estimate is a small statistical claim, and the analysis has to be as trustworthy as the optics.

You also have to be careful about analysing the data. You must always make sure that the localisation algorithm is excellent — test everything. Like any method, you have to validate it.

Section 06

How much should you believe the picture?

The most practical part of the interview: what an expert looks at first when a Nature paper shows him a super-resolution image of a structure.

The question is direct — when you read a paper that uses super-resolution microscopy to study a structure, how much do you believe the images? Moerner calls it a tough question and then answers it as a working scientist would, by naming what he looks for. First, quantification of the photons detected. Second, quantification of how the authors arrived at a resolution number. Those are statistical tests, he says, and they can be done with separate single molecules on your own bench, which is what makes them meaningful rather than decorative.

Then the human checks. Have the authors been trained? Have they learned the technique? Do they use state-of-the-art methods for estimating positions? And finally, the one he stresses hardest: controls. Controls are unbelievably important, and the question a control has to answer is subtle — did attaching the fluorophore change the behaviour of the system you are trying to measure?

He gives a worked example. Suppose you want to resolve a long thin fibril structure and you want the best possible detail, so you pack fluorophores as close together as you can. Past a point they stop behaving as independent sensors. Close fluorophores interact with one another, so they are no longer separate random reporters of position; or they push each other apart and distort the very structure you are measuring. The remedy is procedural: try a different fluorophore, try a very small one as well as your favourite one, and see whether the behaviour is the same.

If you are looking in a regime that has not been observed before with light, you have to always be careful.

Moerner, on new territory
Section 07

IBM Research, and the frequency dimension

How the whole story began: not with microscopy at all, but with an attempt to write many bits into a single spot of light on a storage medium.

Asked how he entered the field, Moerner goes back to the mid-1980s, when he joined IBM Research. The group there was studying optical storage, and the ambition was to store many bits in a single focus spot by using tunability of lasers together with a property of solids at low temperature: their optical absorption is inhomogeneously broadened. He unpacks the term for the audience rather than assuming it — it simply means the absorption profile is not one line but a dense pile of very narrow absorption peaks, one for each molecule or defect, each sitting at a slightly different frequency because each sits in a slightly different local environment.

That spread is what makes frequency usable as a dimension. Instead of one bit per spot, you could write hundreds to a thousand bits in the same spot, choosing the colour at which each one is written and then reading them back by tuning the laser across the profile. One of the hosts offers the analogy of a disc written and read with light, and Moerner takes it further than the analogy: same idea, far smaller scale, and many more bits per spot than a surface technology can hold.

The idea itself dates from the mid-1970s and was pursued at IBM by a group of spectroscopists trying it in every host they could think of — inorganic crystals, glasses, molecules in polymers. Moerner worked mostly on the molecular side. His chosen slice of the problem was not the writing mechanism but the signal-to-noise: how deep a mark can you burn, how strong is the signal, what does the background look like when you try to detect that mark.

The mechanism that makes the marks is spectral hole burning — a technique, he notes dryly, that people do not know much about these days. The hole is the dip left in the absorption profile where you have written a bit, and the question that occupied him was what limits your ability to see it.

Section 08

Spectral holes, and why a broad line is the point

Cooling to liquid helium, turning off the phonons, and using strain as a radio dial — plus the counter-intuitive claim that you want the broadening to be large.

The conversation slows down and Moerner explains why the lines are inhomogeneous in the first place, because everything in the storage scheme depends on it. Cool the sample to liquid-helium temperatures and, for a well-chosen absorber, the individual absorption line becomes extremely narrow: you have turned off the phonons, so there is no longer any phonon scattering to broaden the absorption. Add to that the strains and stresses frozen into the solid, which shift each molecule to a slightly different frequency. The result is a collection of very narrow lines spread across a wide band, fixed in place. He compares it to a radio dial full of stations sitting at different frequencies, all present at once.

Asked whether he would want to reduce that spread, he says the opposite: for this application you want the inhomogeneous broadening to be large, because more spread means more distinct colours per location and therefore more bits. The bits themselves are written by irradiating only the molecules in resonance with the laser — one homogeneous subset — and then making them change: a photochemical reaction, an ionisation, or a small rearrangement of the local environment. Whatever the route, the molecules move to another frequency and leave behind a dip. That dip is the spectral hole.

How long do the marks last? At low temperature very little changes, and the photochemistry need not reverse quickly, so a hole can be permanent for a very long time — subject to being undone deliberately, with heat or with another wavelength, and subject to slow decay for other reasons in a polymer host. Reading is then almost trivially simple: scan the laser and record signal, no signal, signal, no signal. A digital string is read out of one spot.

On line widths, he gives concrete numbers: the homogeneous widths involved were of order eight to ten megahertz. You want them as narrow as possible, because narrower lines pack more bits into the same frequency space, which is one reason crystalline hosts were attractive — polymers are full of low-frequency excitations that stir everything around. And part of the art was chemical: engineering the molecules themselves to produce narrow lines and stable marks.

Section 09

Why it never became a disk drive

A candid account of losing a technology race to a much less elegant competitor that happened to be improving thirty per cent a year.

Did it end up in memory? It did not, and Moerner explains why in terms of competing ideas rather than of his own results. The advantage of the spectral approach would have been spectacular density — a very large number of bits per unit area. Against it stood the requirement for low temperature, which frightened people at the time. He is wry about that in hindsight: today, with so much work in quantum science, plenty of people will happily run at low temperature because the physical effects are worth it.

The decisive competitor, though, was magnetic storage. He calls it the gorilla sitting next to you: a technology improving at roughly thirty per cent compound annual growth, year after year, packing more and more bits into the same device. Drives of the day held about a megabyte. Today the same family of devices holds orders of magnitude more, and that relentless improvement swept away the alternatives — not by being more beautiful, but by being there, and getting better every single year.

It just wiped out all competitors, wiped out all of these side ideas. And so you keep moving. That is the story of technology, really, when you have that amazing technology competing with you.

Moerner, on the gorilla
  • For it: extraordinary areal density, with many hundreds of bits addressed by colour inside one diffraction-limited spot.
  • Against it: liquid-helium temperatures were seen as a serious practical barrier in the 1980s.
  • Against it: it required a whole ecosystem — tunable narrow-line lasers, quantum-limited detectors, frequency-modulation readout.
  • Against it: magnetic storage improved about thirty per cent a year, compounding, and killed the whole family of alternatives.
  • What survived: the physics. Every one of those instrument problems became the tool kit for looking at single molecules a few years later.
Section 10

Dye lasers, and the search for a smooth line

The apparatus of the late 1980s, and the experiment that turned a boring background into a discovery.

The hardware matters here, because the discoveries were made at the edge of what instruments could do. For visible light, the workhorses were tunable dye lasers — tunable single-frequency dye lasers — capable of a line width of a few megahertz at a frequency of five hundred terahertz. Moerner's way of putting it is that this is a very high-Q device for scanning across sharp spectral features. Diode lasers were used too, in situations that suited their narrower tuning ranges and different line widths.

Detection came in two flavours. Some experiments used fluorescence: scan the laser, collect the light that comes out, and record the excitation spectrum. Others measured absorption directly, and there the key technique was frequency-modulation spectroscopy, which Moerner credits to Gary Bjorklund, one of its inventors, and which he describes as working at the quantum limit of sensitivity for transmission measurements. His first experiments used exactly that: transmission, not fluorescence.

On scanning strategy, he is unromantic. You wanted to move over larger ranges, so you used the dye laser itself, which could sweep twenty megahertz quickly, and you did it electronically, with no moving angles. Asked whether he locked to a particular frequency, he says no — they scanned, over and over and over, because what you want from a feature is to see it again and again. Being at IBM Research also gave the group something he values highly: the licence to explore the fundamentals underneath an idea that was being considered for an application, rather than only pushing the application.

Then the urgency of storage re-enters. If you are going to write bits, you had better write them fast — not a second per bit, preferably a nanosecond. So they tried pulses ten nanoseconds long and asked how deep a hole such a pulse could write. The answer was: very shallow. Which pushed the work squarely into the regime of noise, and into the question of the highest-sensitivity measurement they could make on a sample with no bits in it at all.

That measurement — what the absorption line looks like when you have written nothing — is where the conversation goes next, and it is the hinge of the entire episode. Together with Mark Levenson, a key colleague at the time, he set out to measure it as carefully as the technique allowed. Everyone expected a smooth line. It is not smooth.

Section 11

Statistical fine structure

The inhomogeneously broadened line turns out to be structured — and the reason is pure counting statistics.

The expectation was a smooth line: many molecules, uniformly distributed in frequency, adding up to a broad Gaussian with no character of its own. What the careful measurement showed is the opposite. The line is highly structured, and the structure is static — you scan again and you see exactly the same random-looking pattern. Move to a different piece of sample and the pattern is different, as if drawn from a probability distribution. The cause is not any defect of the instrument. It is that the number of molecules falling inside each narrow slice of frequency — each slice the width of one homogeneous line — fluctuates from slice to slice. Those fluctuations have a size of the square root of the number of molecules in the slice.

He named the effect statistical fine structure, and he is affectionately clear about why it is a beautiful thing rather than an annoyance. Put ten times more molecules in and ordinary absorption grows tenfold. This effect does not. Its root-mean-square size grows as the square root of the number in resonance, which makes it one of the very few spectral features of its kind — and it explains, in a single stroke, why small samples looked hopeless: averaging kills it, so anything you build from it must be measured on a small enough sample that the fluctuations survive.

Finding it took two attempts and one decisive change of host. The first samples were polymers, and there the structure refused to hold still: you could see something beginning to repeat, but each repeat looked a little different, never exactly the same. The cause is the low-frequency excitations that polymers are full of — two-level systems that can stir around even at two kelvin and shuffle the molecules from frequency to frequency. Moerner describes himself as getting frustrated and making a decision: forget this, go to a crystalline sample. Pentacene molecules in a para-terphenyl crystal, where those two-level systems cannot exist and everything should be perfectly static.

The sample was made by melting the host on a hot plate and letting it crystallise. Para-terphenyl is the transparent host; pentacene is the guest that absorbs. And the structure, in his words, popped out. Scan it again and the same random-looking pattern is there, identical. Move to another piece of the crystal and the pattern changes, because the next piece is a different draw from the same distribution. In 1987 this was something nobody had observed before.

Section 12

The decision to look at one molecule

Statistical fine structure, run in reverse: if the structure scales as the square root of the count, then the last step to one molecule is only a factor of thirty away.

Having seen something new, the group asked what could be done with it — and here Moerner pauses to recall the advice that shaped the next decade. His managers at IBM told him that he had all these lasers and so forth now, and that he should not get too focused on the application: just do the most exciting science he could think of. What he thought of was to try to see a single molecule. The reasoning runs straight off the fine-structure result. If a thousand molecules in resonance produce a feature of size thirty, then the difficulty of getting down to a single one is not a thousand times greater — it is thirty times greater, because the feature scales as the square root. Thirty times better is achievable.

So, with a postdoc in 1989, they used the same instrumentation that had revealed statistical fine structure, but with a lower concentration of absorbers, and began to see the absorptions of individuals. How do you know it is one molecule? At that stage, honestly, by inference. They simply diluted, and diluted further, and scanned the laser out into the wings of the broad line where fewer and fewer molecules sit, until the individual features appeared so rarely that they had to be spaced far apart. The neighbourhood was emptied, the features were isolated by construction.

Harder proofs came later, and only after the switch from absorption to fluorescence: photon antibunching and bunching, intensity-correlation measurements that can distinguish one emitter from two. He is careful with the epistemology here — some of the evidence is inference, some of it is hard proof — and he notes that at the time nobody had a proof in hand and yet everybody became convinced, because the dilution argument was overwhelming.

That is the first experiment that was very exciting, because no one had done that before. A lot of people thought it was not possible to see single molecules.

Moerner, on 1989
Section 13

“It is too small” — and other reasons people said no

A short, honest accounting of the objections, and of the one instrument that answered them.

Why did people think it could not be done? Moerner's answer is almost colloquial. It is too small — how are we going to detect it? Then there is noise: we see noise all the time, so how do you get rid of it, and what method could possibly make this possible? The method, in his case, was the one he had already been refining for a different purpose: frequency-modulation spectroscopy, which looks at very small spectral features rather than at absolute light levels, and which can detect fractional absorption changes of one part in a hundred thousand and smaller still.

That is the whole trick, and it is worth stating plainly: the obstacle was never the size of a molecule, it was the ratio between a molecule's contribution and everything else in the measurement. Move the measurement to where that ratio lives, and the objection evaporates.

A second class of objection was practical rather than fundamental, and it was about the speed of the eventual application rather than the experiment. If a storage system is to be built from spectral holes, each hole must be written almost instantly — they aimed at ten-nanosecond writing pulses — and such a pulse writes only a shallow hole. That is why the group was so focused on noise limits: not because the physics demanded it, but because the application did. About the philosophical question — was it obvious that any of this would reach biology — he is refreshingly honest: mostly they were thinking that this is pretty cool, not that it would transform cell biology.

Section 14

Switching to fluorescence

From a dark line in a transmission spectrum to light coming out of nothing — the single change that made everything afterwards possible.

A major change came quickly, and it was a change of detector philosophy rather than of physics. The work moved from measuring transmission to measuring fluorescence: instead of looking for a small dip in a large amount of light passing through the sample, you collect light emitted by the molecules themselves. The point is the background. On a transmission measurement you fight everything the beam passes through; on a fluorescence measurement the emitters shine on a dark background, and if you can make the background truly dark, then a single molecule is a bright thing in an empty field.

The early experiments were at one or two kelvin, where the lines are narrow and the physics is clean. Integration times were on the order of a second at first, sometimes a tenth of a second. And the molecule is not a passive object: it is pumped repeatedly, cycling up and down, and every cycle costs you photons. This is where the triplet state first enters the story, as a nuisance. Molecules occasionally make an intersystem crossing into a long-lived triplet state, where they go dark and stay dark. So for these experiments you deliberately chose molecules with a weak triplet yield — molecules that would not disappear on you, but would come back and absorb again and again.

He flags, without developing it yet, that the triplet will return later for entirely different reasons: those spin levels turn out to be usable. But in the beginning, the goal was simply to extract as many photons per molecule per unit time as possible, because that quantity sets the signal and the noise.

If you can get rid of background, if you can have very low background everywhere, then fluorescence is on a dark background.

Moerner, on the decisive change
Section 15

Saturation, and the narrowest line a molecule can have

Sitting on one molecule, turning the power up, and discovering a width limited by nothing but the lifetime of the excited state.

One of the things they could do with a single molecule, which you can never do with an ensemble, is follow it as a function of excitation power. You sit on the molecule, tune across it to measure its line width, then sit on the peak and increase the laser power, watching the signal climb and then stop climbing — saturate. Standard three-level models let you calculate the shape of that curve and the intensity at which the knee occurs, and Moerner calls the exercise fascinating, partly because it worked so well.

The line widths they measured were, in his phrase, lifetime-limited: as narrow as the excited state's lifetime permits, and narrower than anything that dephasing could allow at higher temperature. He points out that this is the same regime that atomic physicists had long enjoyed with sodium in ovens and other atoms in vacuum — an environment so non-interacting that the only width left is the lifetime — with the difference that nobody there had been much concerned with singles.

Asked why a lifetime should set a width at all, he gives the explanation he says he used with students, sometimes successfully. The molecule is an oscillator driven by the laser, cycling up and down. The optical frequency involved is enormous: five hundred terahertz at 590 nanometres. But the molecule can only stay in the excited state for about ten nanoseconds before it emits. An oscillation that is cut off after a finite time cannot be a pure tone; the frequency-domain picture of a truncated oscillation is a spread-out line, and the width is given by the Fourier transform of that truncation. Short life, wide line; long life, narrow line. It is the single most useful piece of intuition in the section, and it explains why the whole enterprise depended on choosing molecules with long-lived excited states and hosts that would let them stay quiet.

That this is the choice point is confirmed later in the interview, when he explains what makes a good fluorophore for this kind of physics: fully allowed singlet-to-singlet electronic transitions with electric dipole character, essentially unity oscillator strength. Such transitions interact with light as well as a molecule can, which is exactly what you need if you want to cycle a single emitter thousands of times per second. Contrast that with, say, rare-earth ions, whose transitions are far weaker and slower, and which require much more intensity to build up any excited-state population.

Section 16

Why pentacene in para-terphenyl

A molecule that stays flat when excited, a host that stays transparent, and a worldwide community of spectroscopists chasing the same narrow lines.

The choice of guest and host was not made from scratch by one group. Pentacene and similar aromatic hydrocarbons were being studied by a whole crew of spectroscopists around the world, several of them inspired by magnetic resonance and trying to reproduce its tricks in the visible — photon echoes and related coherence experiments on absorption lines. For those experiments you want molecules that absorb well and live long in the excited state.

Pentacene has a specific virtue. Put it in the excited state and it stays flat: it does not bend or twist. Many molecules distort when excited, and that distortion changes their electronic properties dramatically, broadening the line and shortening the lifetime. The flat hydrocarbons, held flat by their network of fused rings, keep the narrowest line widths and the longest lifetimes — which, as the previous section explained, is exactly the same as saying they have the narrowest lines. The host, para-terphenyl, is transparent in the visible and can be grown as a clean crystal from a melt, which makes it a quiet lattice for the guest to sit in.

There is also a practical point buried in the conversation: the molecules were being studied anyway, by people with different motivations — absorption spectroscopy, photon echoes, coherence effects — and the super-resolution work inherited their material culture. The specific sample in the 1987 experiment was grown on a hot plate, unglamorously, because what mattered was that the lattice be clean rather than that the method be elegant.

Section 17

What one molecule made possible

Stark shifts, Zeeman shifts, Rabi oscillations — spectroscopy with a sample of exactly one.

Once single molecules could be detected, an obvious question followed: what can you learn from doing spectroscopy on one molecule rather than on an ensemble? Quite a lot, it turned out. Moerner lists the family of experiments that grew from the demonstration: Stark effects, where an electric field shifts the line; Zeeman effects, the magnetic analogue; and, later, coherence experiments, including the Rabi oscillations that a host asks about directly. Groups all over the world showed that these could be done on single molecules.

He also names the obstacle that keeps this from being trivial. For many of these applications you would like the emitters to be at precisely known places in the sample, so that you can address this one and not that one, spatially rather than only in frequency. That is harder, and progress has been partial. The experiments that succeeded first were the fundamental ones, because with single molecules you can move the beam, change the wavelength, and carry on — no cleanroom required. His own summary of the ordering is characteristically blunt: the fundamental experiments come first, applications later.

Asked whether he ever went back from fluorescence to absorption, he gives a general principle about how experimentalists choose problems. People start in an area, move to whatever gives the biggest signals, and then — deliberately — go to places where the signals are worse, in exchange for learning something new. That is what his group did next.

Section 18

Bow ties, and the luck of a good problem

Gold triangles, a molecule in the gap, and the reason a field explodes once it becomes easy enough to join.

The next move away from the strongest signal was into plasmonics: structures that alter the local properties of light on the nanometre scale. The specific device he describes is the bow-tie nano-antenna — two triangles of gold on a surface, their tips almost touching, with a single molecule placed in the gap between them. In the gap the optical field is concentrated into a volume far smaller than a wavelength, and the molecule's absorption and emission properties change accordingly.

He is careful to point out that this is not his private territory any more. Huge arrays of people work on plasmonic antennas today, in many environments, and he offers a perspective that recurs throughout the interview: there are millions of brilliant people in the world thinking of new things to do, which is why fields grow and change rather than arriving complete. And then a comparison that explains the whole shape of his career. In the early days, everything had to be at low temperature, with the cooling, the cryogenics, the sample handling — a genuine limitation on who could participate. The narrowest lines belong to the coldest samples. So the question that mattered was how to escape the cold.

Section 19

Single molecules at room temperature

In the mid-1990s the frequency dimension — the one that had made the storage scheme work — had to be given up, and the work got bigger as a result.

It was really exciting in the mid-1990s, he says, when people started detecting single molecules at room temperature — several groups, in Japan and the United States and elsewhere, arriving at the same capability from different directions. The price is stated immediately: you no longer have frequency control over which molecules are in resonance. Everything broadens, and roughly every molecule absorbs over the same range. Selection by colour is gone.

What is left is selection by dilution, taken much further than before. You dilute by another factor of ten, or a thousand, or ten thousand, or a hundred thousand, until on average only one molecule sits inside the focal volume — and the focal volume here means ordinary diffraction-limited optics, which he casually identifies with the red light of the series name, halved. One emitter in that volume, plus sufficient sensitivity, plus a truly dark background: that is the whole recipe. He notes the practical caveat too — you have to be sure there is not a lot of other junk emitting nearby.

The proof that a signal really comes from one emitter also becomes routine here. Intensity correlation is one way, as the host suggests. Another is simply to watch the signal over time, and that is where the interview turns, because the observation made in 1997 was a surprise.

Section 20

Blinking, 1997

A single green fluorescent protein turning itself on and off — seen first as an oddity, later understood as the mechanism that unlocked super-resolution.

They had seen it at low temperature before, but in 1997 they saw it at room temperature: the emission from a single green fluorescent protein turned on and off. It blinks, spontaneously, driven by the optical excitation and by photochemistry in the absorber. Moerner explains why the observation is qualitatively different from anything an ensemble can do. With a huge number of molecules the signal decays smoothly, like an exponential. With one molecule there is nothing to average: the signal goes off in a single step, and then, often, comes back. Off and on and off and on. Digital, not analogue.

He remembers the moment in the lab with the affection of someone who has told this story often. His office was across the hall from the lab, and the postdoc came running in: the molecules are jumping around. That phrase — molecules jumping around — had belonged to the low-temperature work, where molecules were seen to wander from one frequency to another. Now it meant something else: at room temperature, in San Diego, another worker was seeing the same on-and-off process in a different system.

The reaction from parts of the community was dismissive, and he quotes it without rancour: these people working on single molecules are wasting their time, because the molecules are not stable, they turn on and off, they blink. Moerner's position was the opposite. The blinking was a physical effect that needed understanding — what is going on, why is this happening — and, in the meantime, other people had already noticed something more useful: the blinking itself could be used to achieve super-resolution.

It is a huge area of growth for people to think about ways to control this process. I was excited about this physical effect.

Moerner, on the sceptics

What he describes, in other words, is the moment a nuisance became an instrument. Blinking is not a defect that the field tolerated; it is the mechanism on which the entire single-molecule super-resolution industry now runs, because the randomness that ruins a clean measurement hands you one molecule at a time, which is exactly what you need to separate emitters whose images overlap.

Section 21

Controlling the blink

It can be complicated or it can be simple — some blinking yields to chemistry, some to light, and one trick turns binding itself into the switch.

Asked what blinking is and whether it can be controlled, Moerner gives the least tidy answer in the whole interview, and means it: it can be complicated, or it can be simple, and it can be controlled in certain cases and not so easily in others. What he can do is sort the attempts into families. One family turns molecules on with light. Another turns them off with chemistry. A third borrows the binding and unbinding of two molecules as the switch itself.

The light-activated family is the one Betzig built. Use molecules that are normally not emitting; shine a different colour on them, blue perhaps, and they begin absorbing and emitting — an on switch for the molecule. If you do that with sufficiently low intensity, you can turn on only one. So you start with none, turn on one, measure its position, turn on another, measure its position, turn on another — and even though they sit at different random places, you can measure all of their positions. Moerner calls that the key idea for super resolution with single molecules.

The second family is chemistry, and it is the one he describes as the STORM-style trick. You add a molecule to the sample that can turn the emitters off, but reversibly. Keep radiating the sample and they spontaneously come back on by themselves. Then dial in the concentration until the number that are on at any instant settles at a low level — so that only one is on at a given time. Nothing about the optics changes; the statistics of who is lit changes.

The third family is the most elegant, because it does not add a separate on-off agent at all: it uses the act of binding. DNA handles, molecules that are dim in solution but bright the moment they attach. Moerner traces the idea back to Robin Hochstrasser, who used a molecule that brightens when it binds to the membrane of a cell — dim while it is floating, bright and therefore localisable while it is docked, then released, and free again for the next one to take its place. The acronym in use is PAINT, and he cheerfully admits it is complicated.

In all three cases the price is the same, and it is worth naming: what you buy is duration. The molecules are submitting to being sampled one at a time, frame after frame, and the information is assembled serially afterwards. You are not photographing the structure; you are interrogating it, repeatedly, and only then drawing a picture.

So you start with none and then turn on one and measure it, measure its position, turn on another, measure its position … even if they are at different random positions, you can measure all their positions. That is the key idea for super resolution with single molecules.

Moerner, on photoactivation
Section 22

Super localisation is not super resolution

Seeing one molecule is not the same as telling two apart — and almost everything else follows from that distinction.

There is a distinction Moerner insists on before anything else, because he thinks it is the source of a great deal of confusion. Seeing a single molecule by itself is not super resolution. He would call it super localisation. If there is only one emitter in the field of view, then of course you can measure the shape of the spot it makes and determine its position with extraordinary precision — how precisely is a statistical matter, fixed by how many photons you manage to collect from it. What single molecules alone cannot do is separate two emitters whose spots sit on top of one another.

True super resolution therefore needed something extra: a way to make the molecules take turns. That is what blinking supplies, and it is why the effect raised in the previous sections — the one that made some people dismiss single-molecule work as unusable — became the mechanism the whole field now stands on. One molecule lit, one position measured, then darkness again; repeat a hundred thousand times; a picture sharper than the optics that made it.

The instrument has also changed shape. Where the earlier low-temperature experiments scanned a laser, this work is done on a wide-field microscope — a whole field imaged at once. The ideas do not change: the diffraction limit and the point spread function still govern a wide-field microscope just as they govern a confocal one. Every object that turns on writes a small blob onto the detector, diffraction-limited in its width, and the job is to find the centre of that blob as well as the photons allow. More photons, better accuracy. The change is that the camera watches many molecules at once and hopes, at every instant, that at most one of them in a given small patch is on — because you do not want too many things on at the same time; they overlap, and then you cannot localise them any more.

How you find the centre is, in his phrase, a whole estimation problem. You can find the peak; you can fit the blob; you can run a maximum-likelihood analysis and ask where the emitter most probably was. All of those are legitimate, and all of them are part of the process of finding the position of a molecule.

There is a counter-intuitive detail about pixels that he explains patiently, because it is the first thing newcomers get wrong. The spot should not be squeezed onto one sensor element; you deliberately spread the diffraction-limited spot over several of them — he says six, or five, or nine. Each pixel is then measuring a different part of the shape, and the different photon counts across the pixels are precisely the information you fit. But the appetite for pixels has a limit. Spread the same photons over more and more elements and there are fewer and fewer photons per element, until you are down among the dark counts and other processes generating bogus photons that are not helping you. So there is a sweet spot, and practitioners find it.

One consequence of that last point deserves its own sentence, because it is the reason single-molecule work tells you anything about biology at all. The molecule is a few nanometres across; the spot it produces on the camera is a few hundred nanometres across, because diffraction smears it. All the biology happens at the small scale and all the data arrives at the large one. If you can extract more information about where the emitter sits — its position, and later its orientation — then you learn more about its local environment, and about what surrounds it.

Section 23

Where the precision comes from

Not from better glass: the objective is already as good as physics allows. Precision is bought with photons, or with a cleverer shape.

What limits the spatial resolution, the host asks. Moerner separates the problem cleanly. On the optics side there is very little left to win: objectives generally produce a diffraction-limited image, and they are the best they can be, so there is not a lot anyone can do to go better in that direction. Everything else hangs on the second term — how many photons you recorded. Each photon is a measurement of the shape of the spot. So the precision with which you know the position improves as one over the square root of the number of photons detected. Four times the photons, twice the precision. This is the second appearance of the square-root law in the interview, and it arrives in a completely different costume.

There is a newer family of ideas for beating that curve, and they are elegantly simple in principle. Put a donut beam — a beam with a dark centre, the same object as in the STED section — right on top of the molecule, so that it emits nothing at all. Now move a tiny distance and you suddenly get good information about where it is. Position becomes something you infer from a null. Moerner has been working on this himself and calls the whole scheme a way of determining position more precisely with fewer photons, which, since photons are the currency, is the only kind of improvement that matters.

He also adds a caution that belongs here rather than in the previous section, because it is about what the measurement means rather than how precise it is. Through all of this you are sensing the average position of an electric dipole — the emitter announcing itself by its oscillating dipole field. Nothing in that measurement is telling you about individual atoms inside the molecule. He returns to this point much later, when someone asks what a precision of a tenth of a nanometre really signifies, and the answer he gives — the average position of the whole object — is set up here.

Section 24

What was actually new

Five, ten, fifty times better than a diffraction-limited microscope is not an incremental improvement — it is a different dataset, and sometimes a different phenomenon.

Was there a hero experiment, something that startled people outside the single-molecule community? Moerner gives both halves of the answer, and the order matters. The first half is quantitative. If you can measure with resolution five, ten or fifty times better than a diffraction-limited microscope — down to ten nanometres or so rather than the hundred to two hundred nanometres of ordinary optics — then you have dramatically more information about the system: where things are, what shapes they have. The corollary he states plainly is that all previous imaging of biological systems should be repeated, so that you can see what else was there that was missed when you did not have the resolution to see it.

The second half is about phenomena rather than precision, and it is the part he enjoys more. What was seen that no one had seen at all? His first example comes from a group studying proteins in axons — the long fibres that run from a neuron's cell body out to its terminus. In axon sections, a particular protein forms ring-like bands, spaced closely together but on the order of a hundred nanometres apart. That banding simply cannot be observed without super resolution. It was not a sharper version of something already known; it was something absent from the record.

His second example is collaborative and came out of wanting to answer a biological question rather than a physical one: work with the microbiologist Lucy Shapiro on proteins inside bacteria. The point of the study was that bacteria are not simply bags of enzymes. They contain internal structures that matter for how they divide, and those structures had been invisible.

Then there is the class of results that is not about resolution at all. If you want to watch something really fast, conventional super resolution can be the wrong tool, because building up the locations takes time and you do not want the molecules moving while you build. So you drop to the single-molecule limit instead: dilute even further, take the resolution hit, and simply watch one molecule. What you see is a distinctive rhythm — a molecule moves, then stops, then starts again, then stops. Some of the stopping is a binding site holding it; some is ordinary diffusion interrupting itself. Measure the on-times and off-times and how they depend on concentration and you extract the kinetics directly, just by watching single molecules. You can watch molecular motors step along their tracks and measure the size of the steps. You can watch how chromosomes move inside the nucleus under different conditions. None of this required a sharper picture; it required a sample sparse enough that individual objects could be followed.

Molecules will move around and then they will stop moving. Then they start moving again … so you measure those on and off times, and the dependence on concentration. You get the kinetics directly just by watching the single molecule motions.

Moerner, on dynamics
Section 25

One virus, a thousand copies

The static picture Moerner showed his hosts: the replication machinery of SARS-CoV-2, mapped by blinking.

Partway through the conversation Moerner shows a picture and asks for a moment to explain it, because it needs explanation. In the middle there is a large black round region: the nucleus of a cell, with no fluorophores in it at all. Around it, and only around it, sit many small spots that resolve into little clusters. The image was made with the blinking method described above — the ordinary super-resolution workflow, applied to something that is very much not a toy sample.

What the picture shows is SARS-CoV-2 infecting a mammalian cell. It is an RNA virus, and once inside it must make many, many copies of its genome. The clusters are where that happens: they are essentially replication organelles, small organelles effectively manufactured by hijacking the machinery of the host cell. Inside each one there are, by his estimate, hundreds to a thousand or so copies of the viral RNA, together with an enzyme that is copying that RNA.

The detail he seems to find most striking is the disproportion between cause and effect. The entire infection came from a single virus particle carrying a single copy of the viral RNA. From that one starting molecule the cell is reorganised into dozens of dense factories, each of them visible only because the RNA inside can be labelled and the labels can be made to blink. He is explicit that this is a static measurement and not a movie: it is a snapshot of a structure that was invisible before, not a recording of the process.

What the clusters are, and are not

The bright blobs are clusters of localised RNA, not pixels that count molecules. The number — hundreds to a thousand copies per organelle — comes from the biology of the replication compartment, and Moerner gives it as such. The measurement supplies the geometry: where the compartments sit, how tightly the RNA is packed inside them, and how completely they surround the nucleus.

Section 26

The label problem

Fluorescence is exquisitely sensitive and completely blind. Something has to tell it what it is looking at — and that something can change the answer.

Then the host asks the obvious follow-up: if you want to see a protein, you must stick a fluorophore on it — so how was that figured out, and what does it cost? Moerner calls it one of the challenges of the whole area. You do have to make the target fluorescent, which means attaching a fluorescent molecule to the molecule or the protein of interest. That is a little bit of chemistry, and there are many techniques for doing it. Some attach an entire fluorescent protein, three to five nanometres in size. Some of the newer techniques attach something much smaller, carrying a single fluorescent molecule.

The crucial word is assay, which he borrows from biology. You have to develop the label so that it attaches to what you want to study without perturbing it. Get it wrong and you may alter the behaviour you were trying to observe — and then the beautiful image is a picture of your own reagent. Everybody has to watch for that, in every experiment, and he returns to the theme several times: controls are unbelievably important. Do you know that putting the fluorophore on has not changed how the system behaves? You may have to change the fluorophore. You may have to try a thin one, or a very small one, alongside your favourite, and check whether the behaviour is the same.

The alternative is to do without labels, and here he is honest about the state of the art. There is label-free work going on, including in his own laboratory, in the family of interferometric scattering microscopies. It is a real technique and it is advancing, but it is still at something like a hundred and twenty nanometres — not yet a single molecule, and not yet a substitute for a label that declares what a structure is. Single molecules have been seen with scattering and with photothermal methods, but those are quite different approaches. He calls label-free work perhaps one of the big things that is going to change in the future, and does not pretend it has changed yet.

He also explains why the labels are not merely a convenience. Imagine looking inside a cell with an electron microscope: you see electron density, and electron density is everywhere. Everything lights up. There are grey blobs and, sometimes, linear structures that turn out to be actin filaments or microtubules or other components. If you want to say which protein a structure is made of — to specify it — you attach a fluorescent label, light that one thing up, and thereby identify it. Without a label, as he puts it, good luck specifying it.

You do have to make it be fluorescent … you have to develop the assay so that you have a good label to attach, and you do not want to perturb it. Controls are unbelievably important.

Moerner, on labelling
Section 27

Two microscopes, one sample

The optical microscope says which protein and where. The electron microscope says what shape it is. Getting both means a sample that survives both.

The way to have both kinds of answer is to use both instruments on the same specimen, and Moerner describes a correlative experiment he took part in. Take a sample. Cool it down. Measure the positions of single molecules attached to one particular protein. Then carry that same sample, still in liquid nitrogen, up to the electron microscope, measure the electron image, and put the fluorescence on top of it. The two pictures are of the same object, so the labelled points can be read against the anatomical detail.

The return to low temperature in this project is not a revival of the early physics for its own sake. The reason is the electron microscope: the electron beam slowly destroys the sample as it images it, and low temperature is what protects it. That is why the group went back to cold experiments — not because the lines are narrow there, but because the specimen has to survive a second instrument.

Plunge-freezing is the hinge of the workflow. Dipping the sample into liquid ethane vitrifies the water rather than crystallising it, so the water becomes amorphous and transparent. That is what makes it possible to do optical microscopy on a frozen specimen: single-molecule positions, blinking or not blinking as required, super-resolution localisations where the density is high. Then the same sample stays in liquid nitrogen and is carried to the electron microscope, loaded cold, and measured tomographically. He describes the whole sequence and then adds, with the tone of someone who has run it, that it can certainly be simplified in future.

It also changes the demands on the emitters. Now you want more control of the molecules: to make them blink in different ways, on when you want them on and off when you want them off, steered by light rather than merely tolerated. The cold turns out to help here rather than hinder. At low temperatures some of those switching processes can be modulated with light, so the same device can be driven with more than one colour — in the simplest version, one colour to read out, another to switch off, another to switch on.

Section 28

Making the emitter say more

If labels cannot be abolished, they can at least be made more talkative: spectra now, vibrations next.

Could you engineer the cell itself to be easier to image, the host wonders — build in structures that fluoresce, make the labelling problem trivial? Moerner likes the idea and says it would be fantastic if someone found a good way to do it. He then points out how much specificity is already achieved without any engineering: antibodies, objects with a shape that binds only one kind of protein, are in general use precisely because they give you a very specific interaction.

His third strand is the one with the most room left in it. If you could also modify the properties of the emitters themselves, that could be very cool, and people have started to. Spectra of single objects and single molecules are now being measured, meaning the wavelength distribution of the light coming out of one emitter is recorded rather than merely its arrival. The spectrum tells you something about the local environment: whether the molecule is in one kind of site or another, or has been shifted to another wavelength. That is an extra variable, and every extra variable is a new way to tell things apart when positions alone are not enough.

Beyond that, he describes more sophisticated spectroscopies in which specific vibrational modes are excited and then the light coming from that vibration is collected — using multiple laser colours, in the general family of coherent Raman techniques. He calls it a beautiful nonlinear method, and is clear about the difficulty: it is not easy to push to the single-molecule level, though there has been some progress. It is exactly the kind of thing he means when he says elsewhere that when you want to know what the molecule is, vibrations are a more direct answer than brightness.

Section 29

The whole cell at once

Thousands of proteins, all moving: the dream, and an honest accounting of how far away it still is.

The host lays out the dream and asks about the road to it: a cell is a huge number of proteins and other macromolecules interacting at once, and one would like to know where they all are, watch the movie unfold, and finally see what is going on. Moerner agrees that even today it is still fairly hazy what happens in a cell, and then answers in layers.

On multiplicity there is real progress. People are working on highly multiplexed imaging and making great progress with barcodes; it is an area of current research, pushing for more and more information. He adds a trade that people outside the field often miss: sometimes a lower resolution is perfectly acceptable. If the ambition is to see the whole cell, investigators are often content with resolution that is not down at the nanometre scale, and that is what makes the problem tractable at all.

On dynamics combined with multiplicity, he is blunt. If you want to see everything all at once, that is still out of reach. You can watch certain things moving, on certain time scales, at certain densities. But if the density is very high, and everything is moving around, that is quite a tough problem. He notes that some people are going to attempt it with machine learning, and then says what such an attempt would require: a lot of orthogonal measurements, which is to say several independent kinds of data that constrain the same object from different directions, so that something can be pulled apart out of the mess.

That last remark is the most useful sentence in the section, because it is a statement about method rather than about ambition. A crowded, moving sample is not solved by more of the same measurement; it is solved by combining measurements that fail in different ways.

Section 30

Is fluorescence the future?

Sensitivity down to a single molecule — and the uncomfortable truth that the field exploded because the method was easy.

Will fluorescence still be the workhorse, or will something displace it? Moerner declines to make a prediction and instead widens the definition. Fluorescence is no longer only the absorption and emission of small molecules. People are working with nanoparticles that have different colours and different spectral characteristics, which can be fascinating and very useful, and which last much longer than a single molecule will — with one obstacle: you have to make them blink. Other tricks are beginning to do exactly that. All of these ideas, he says, are being thrown together in every way possible by brilliant people all over the world, and it is very exciting at the moment.

He then makes the observation about the field that perhaps explains more than any technical argument. Fluorescence has extreme sensitivity — down to one molecule, easily. But part of its success is not sensitivity at all: it is that the method is relatively easy. That is precisely why so many people all over the world could start doing it, and do it, and keep doing it, until the field expanded dramatically. Had every single experiment been very difficult, the growth would have been nothing like as explosive. Accessibility, in other words, is a scientific force.

The thing about fluorescence is that it does have the extreme sensitivity down to one molecule easily. But it is partly because it is relatively easy — that is why so many people all over the world could start doing this, and make the field expand dramatically.

Moerner, on why the field grew

Set beside his earlier remarks about the sheer number of clever people entering the area, the point becomes a kind of structural theory of the subject. A technique that is merely the best will be used by the few who can build it. A technique that is good enough, and easy enough, will be used by everyone, and will therefore discover more — including the things its inventor never thought to look for.

Section 31

The third dimension

A microscope records a plane. Putting depth back in — and orientation with it — means deliberately reshaping the spot.

Almost everything discussed so far happens in a plane. A standard microscope records an image, and an image is X and Y; you would like Z as well. Moerner says a number of people have added the Z dimension in various ways, and describes the one his group has worked on with collaborators, which is a genuinely odd idea: instead of changing the sample or the optics, change the microscope's own function. Add components outside the microscope that rearrange the light coming out of it into a different pattern. Instead of emerging as a single point, the light leaves as two spots that revolve around one another as the depth changes, so depth is written into the angle of the pair. Trios of spots that rotate, and other shapes, encode it too. He sounds slightly delighted that a microscope can be modified like that at all.

The story comes with a keepsake. One of his postdocs, Yoav Shechtman, left him a picture with two halves: on one side the phase pattern that is impressed on the light, and on the other his own face, made by processing the image of a single emitter through that pattern. A single molecule's spot, re-aimed, draws a portrait.

Shape-changing has a second payoff, which is orientation. The light coming out of the molecule has a dipole pattern, because the emitter is a dipole; the information is physically there in the emission. Most microscopes throw it away — they smear over it and make a spot. If instead you image through polarisers, often splitting into two channels, two polarisations, then the shapes in the two channels differ, provided the detection has been designed for it. Add phase patterns on top and you can modify the light further still. Moerner describes the net effect as remixing the modes of the electromagnetic field that correspond to that spot — the modes that make up the point spread function — and using the remix to report orientation as well as position. Several members of his group have designed such patterns, including Matt Lew, whom he names among his best students and postdocs.

What you are doing is remixing the modes of the electromagnetic field that correspond to that spot, and using that to tell you orientations as well as position.

Moerner, on shape-changing detection

There is an older practical matter that runs underneath all of this: collecting the most photons you can. That is why immersion oil is used, to reach a higher numerical aperture — without it you are limited in the angular range from which light can be gathered, and since precision is bought with photons, a lost photon is a lost unit of information.

Section 32

Spins, and the second life of the triplet state

A state that was once a nuisance — the dark trap that stole photons — becomes a handle for radio waves, magnetic fields, and eventually quantum control.

The question is whether the group ever used the spin levels of the molecules, with radio-frequency or microwave excitation. The answer is yes, and it goes straight back to the triplet state that was such an annoyance in the early fluorescence experiments. Put a pentacene molecule at low temperature and cycle it in the singlet states: every so often intersystem crossing takes it into the triplet. That is the dark state that used to cost photons. But the triplet has structure that the singlet does not. Because of the way the molecule is built, its spin sublevels are already split even in zero magnetic field — three levels sitting very close together whenever the molecule is in the triplet.

Give the molecule radio waves at the right frequency and you can drive transitions between those sublevels. Around 1993, in his group and in parallel work in France, the experiment was optically detected magnetic resonance: keep cycling the molecule and collecting its fluorescence, then tune the microwave to move population between the spin sublevels — which changes the amount of light coming out. The fluorescence becomes an optical readout of a spin transition. Moerner calls it the beginning of spins, and notes that it opened a whole area in which people manipulate these spins in different ways.

That area is now much larger than molecules, and he follows it where it goes. The same idea is heavily used today for potential quantum effects in nitrogen-vacancy centres and other defect centres — and, lately, back in molecules, and even in fluorescent proteins: a paper published not long ago reported optically detected magnetic resonance in a green fluorescent protein. In the early experiments, he adds, there were many such proteins rather than one.

Asked whether the spins are used for measuring fields rather than merely for adjusting emission, he says yes, absolutely, and explains the mechanism precisely. The spectra in radio-frequency space carry signatures of hyperfine interactions — couplings between the electron spin and nearby nuclear spins — and those couplings change when the local environment changes. So a single molecule's spin levels can report on the magnetic fields immediately around it. The same structure that makes the molecules hard to observe becomes a local sensor.

The triplet state, twice

Early in the interview the triplet is a defect to be designed around: molecules with a strong triplet yield go dark and disappear on you, so you choose ones with a weak yield and take as many photons per second as you can get. Decades later the same state is the point of the experiment, because its zero-field split sublevels can be addressed with radio waves. Nothing about the molecule changed — only what the experimenter wanted from it.

Section 33

How far can it go?

Tenth-of-a-nanometre claims are real — and they are still the average position of a dipole, not a picture of atoms.

The horse-race question comes up again: is the competition among super-resolution methods still running, and what are the territories now? Moerner's first move is a disclaimer — he says he is not really the right person, because he is not doing the horse race. Then he acknowledges what the racers have achieved. He sees papers by really good groups getting down to a tenth of a nanometre resolution, and they prove it in various ways to show that it is valid. That, he says, sounds fantastic, and it is.

What he will not do is guess the endpoint; asked how far it will go, he says he does not know. Instead he spends the section on interpretation, which is where the real content is. Through all of this you are always measuring an average position. You sense the molecule through its electric dipole. You are not getting information about the individual atoms inside the emitter, and the reason lies in what an excited molecule actually is: a set of molecular orbitals whose wave functions are spread across the whole molecule, which, after a while, converts — he says the word bang — into a far-field propagating electromagnetic wave that travels out to the detectors. There is no atom-by-atom reporter in that process. So what a tenth of a nanometre means, he explains, is the average position of the whole object, or more exactly of the position of that dipole field. It is an extremely sharp answer to a question about the centre of a spread-out thing.

People are trying to get past that limit by other means, and he is careful about where those means lead. They often quickly find themselves in other kinds of physics: tunnelling microscopes and very small tips brought extremely close, where by looking at currents you can see the bonds in an individual molecule and the shape it makes. That is a real capability, he says, and it is a totally different kind of experiment — not a better version of this one.

It is not sensing individual atoms. It means the average position of the whole thing — of the position of that electric dipole field.

Moerner, on tenth-of-a-nanometre precision
Section 34

What he tells people starting out

Learn one field properly, stay willing to be the least knowledgeable person in the room, and treat failure as information.

Asked what he would tell someone beginning a scientific career — industry first, as he did, or something else — Moerner declines to hand out a route and gives a habit instead. The right thing is to learn a field very well, so that you are genuinely an expert in it. Then he adds the qualification that shaped his own training: study your subject with a mind to learning about other fields as well. His graduate work sat at the boundary between physics and chemistry, which meant he got both, and the particular problem was vibrational modes of molecules in a solid, measured with infrared light.

Being willing to be ignorant is the part he emphasises most, and he says it plainly: you have to be prepared to be not so smart, to not know everything. Start working with biologists and they will know a great deal more biology than you do, so you learn it. You have to be comfortable making mistakes at the beginning, because that is how learning happens.

On the industrial path itself he is clarifying rather than recommending. IBM Research, where he went, was a special kind of industrial laboratory — he places it beside Bell Laboratories and the General Motors research laboratories — a place where people could do fundamental work, publish it, and be rewarded for doing really exciting science. The research division was meant to be famous for its science and technology; some colleagues were superb technologists, and others interested in fundamental questions also did well. He calls that environment a continuation of university fundamental work, with one difference: there were problems in view, storage among them, and wanting to invent new ways to store data guided the research in a certain direction.

Then he states the principle he has already implicitly recommended twice in the conversation — about putting fields together. If you can combine several fields, you have more insight into where the new things are and where the boundaries lie. And he ends on failure, at length, as though it matters more than the rest.

Make sure that if you fail at something, that you learn something from it. Failure is a learning process. If you turn it into a process where you ask why did this fail, what did I not understand, what do I need to learn — then it all turns into a positive experience.

Moerner, to people starting out

He adds what worries him: people who become too unhappy when something fails, instead of being glad of it, because the failure is about to teach them something. The advice is not consolation. It is the description of a working method, from someone whose own subject exists only because a nuisance effect turned out to be the mechanism.

Section 35

Sputnik kid

A book about radios, a father who knew electronics, unsoldered televisions — and four years of high-school Russian.

How did he get into science in the first place? He answers with the historical situation first. He was a Sputnik kid: he grew up while there was a huge effort in the United States, and a widespread concern, that people were not learning enough science. Part of the impetus to study science came from that environment — not a command, he insists, but a nudge in a certain direction.

The other half of the answer is domestic. His parents supported everything he wanted to do, in science and in music. His father worked with and knew a lot about electronics, and had taken a physics degree as an undergraduate without going on to graduate work. He gave his son a book about radios. Moerner studied it, became interested in radios, and became a ham radio operator.

From there he describes an environment rather than an event, and it is the part he clearly thinks matters. A world in which people tinker and build things: taking apart the huge old television sets that households had in those days and unsoldering every component in them. Doing things with your hands, being encouraged to learn about all of it. That is the kind of backdrop, he says, that is very helpful to success in science.

The cultural detail is nice and he gives it freely. He studied Russian in high school — four years of it — because at the time it seemed a good language to learn. Others were learning German and Italian for the same reason, and of course Spanish, growing up in San Antonio, was essential.

The closing thought is a worry, and it is the one place where the interview turns wistful. The essential thing, he says, is caring about how things work — and he is concerned that we have less of that now. We all carry around remarkable computers and many people have no idea what is inside them, even though there is an enormous amount of science in there. It is not too complex; you can learn about every part of it. The obstacle is practical: components have become very small, you can no longer see the wires, and unsoldering something to see how it works has got harder. He allows that you can now buy a quite good little USB microscope, but points out that even that will not let you get inside a chip. You can still go to school and learn about every one of those components, he adds, and that is a pretty exciting thing.
