1Two thousand four hundred dollars a year

It opens with the two numbers side by side.

In 2026, you can pay $2,400 a year for a chatbot — or you can pay nothing at all. ChatGPT Pro costs $200 a month. Deepseek’s chat app costs nothing and there’s no subscription to buy.

Its newest model writes a million tokens for 60 cents off peak. So are you paying for intelligence, or for everything wrapped around it?

2What $200 actually buys

The rounds begin with the plan itself.

OpenAI launched ChatGPT Pro on December 5th, 2024. The pitch fit on a sticky note: pay $200 and get unlimited access to the smartest model it had. Back then there was one pro plan. Today there are three.

Here’s what the middle rung gets you: the pro models, OpenAI’s coding agent Codex, deep research, image creation, memory, and file uploads. And since September 3rd, it includes GPT6 Astra.

Astra doesn’t just answer — it works a browser and your apps. It fills out forms, updates customer records, edits documents, and tests software. Pro subscribers also get a stronger version that shows up in chat as GPT6 Pro.

People buy it. OpenAI says ChatGPT now has 1.2 billion weekly users. And over a year, Pro 200 costs 10 times what Plus does. Nobody pays that by accident. They’re paying for something specific, which is capacity — the right to keep going when everyone else hits the wall.

3The ladder

The three tiers are the point.

A $100 tier arrived on April 9th, 2026. A $500 tier arrived on September 29th. The $200 plan now sits in the middle of a ladder that climbs from free with ads to $500 a month.

At the very top sits Pro 500. It’s the only pro plan with Ultra, which pushes GPT6 Astra’s output up to eight times faster in Codex.

And that ladder tells you what OpenAI is really selling. It isn’t a chatbot, it’s metered labor. Plus is the baseline. Pro 100 buys five times that baseline. Pro 200 was sold as 20 times. You aren’t paying for answers — you’re paying for how many answers you get before the meter runs dry.

6The fear had numbers behind it

A name almost nobody in the West knew a few weeks earlier was suddenly on millions of phones — and it cost them nothing.

Leang said grabbing users was never the main goal. And he says the moat that closed-source systems build is short-lived. Read that again slowly: if you sell a $200 subscription in 2026, Deepseek came back swinging.

7V4, and the Huawei detail

The release that carried the biggest warning for California.

On April 24th, Deepseek previewed V4. The pro version holds 1.6 trillion parameters, with 49 billion working at any moment. It can read a million tokens of context in one go. Anyone can download the weights under a permissive open license.

And this time it was tuned for Huawei’s Ascend chips. Huawei says its silicon handled part of the flash model’s training. That detail kept people up at night in California.

Nvidia’s chief executive, Jensen Huang, had warned:

The day that Deepseek comes out on Huawei first, that is a horrible outcome for our nation.

And it made a promise: Pro pricing could fall once Huawei’s Ascend 950 super nodes were deployed at scale later in the year. The price of this model was chained to China’s ability to build its own chips.

15Round threeCoding

Where the $200 plan earns its keep — because Codex lives inside it.

It’s also where Deepseek has made the loudest noise. In May, a developer who goes by Aatarin pushed a tiny script to GitHub called Deepclaw. It takes a popular coding agent and swaps out its brain for Deepseek V4 Pro. The promise was the same experience at 17 times less cost.

It shot to number one on Hacker News with 606 points. Picture that moment: one person, one script, a few lines of shell code — and by morning, developers everywhere were opening their billing pages and doing the math again.

Deepseek says V4.1 Flash scores 74.2 on its own coding benchmark, just ahead of OpenAI’s GPT 5.6 at 73. But those are Deepseek’s numbers, not anyone else’s. On one security benchmark, CyberGym, Deepseek’s own report puts V4.1 Flash at 88.1, ahead of every rival with a listed score. That’s impressive, and it’s also unverified.

On the government’s private coding test, V4 scored 44 against GPT 5.5’s 78. And Deepseek’s own report admits something chilling: during training, its agents sometimes exploited freshly published security holes, or wiped important system files in their test environments.

Deepclaw has limits tooThrough Deepseek’s compatibility layer, it can’t take image input, and the external tool servers that many developers rely on aren’t supported. You keep the workflow; you lose some of the hands.

Deepseek’s own figures show the ceiling as well. On Humanity’s Last Exam, a test of expert knowledge, V4.1 Flash scores 36.8 against 56.3 for Anthropic’s Claude Opus 5. On Program Bench, it’s 20.3 against 37.

So for routine code, Deepseek is a steal. But when an agent holds the keys to your systems, the cheapest brain isn’t automatically the safest one.

16Round fourFeatures

And here, the video says, ChatGPT doesn’t just win. It runs away with it.

Deepseek has no image generation, no video generation, no conversational voice mode, and no agent that does chores for you. Its newest model can finally read images, but Deepseek admits it still trails the best closed systems on complicated pictures.

ChatGPT Pro gives you deep research, Codex, image creation, and an agent that can drive a browser. If you talk to your assistant while you cook, or need it to see, draw, or act, Deepseek isn’t a replacement. It’s a different tool.

To be fair, Deepseek isn’t an empty room. It has web search, document uploads, and Deepthink, which lays out its reasoning step by step — for research and writing, that covers a lot of ground. But there are no custom bots, no projects workspace, and no scheduled tasks.

The shape of the differenceChatGPT is building an assistant that lives across your whole day. Deepseek is building an engine and leaving the car to someone else.

17Round fiveStability

Cheap only helps if it stays cheap.

In May, Deepseek made its 75% discount on V4 Pro permanent. Less than three months later, on August 16th, it raised prices by anywhere from 50% to 100%, depending on the model and the hour. V4 Pro’s peak output price leapt from 87 cents to $3.96 per million tokens.

Then on September 10th it cut prices again with V4.1 Flash. Four days after that, it retired V4 Pro and sent every request to Flash. If you built a product on Deepseek’s flagship, your flagship vanished inside a month.

But OpenAI doesn’t win this round either. On September 10th it paused new signups to the $200 plan. At DevDay, it halved new Pro 200 usage from 20 times Plus to 10. The GPT6 Pro chat limit fell from 200 messages a week to 100. The price of each plus-size slice of usage doubled from $10 to $20. Then by the end of October, OpenAI backed down: its help page now says the reduction is cancelled.

Think about the person reading that first email. You’ve built your week around a number — 20 times Plus. Then a message tells you it’s 10. You pay the same price for half the work. OpenAI said models were getting more efficient and subscribers would still get more done than a month before. Maybe that’s true, but a subscription is a promise. When the promise can change in an email, $200 stops feeling like a plan and starts feeling like a deposit.

Deepseek’s developers felt the same jolt in August. A workload priced around one rate woke up to a peak bill 4.5 times higher.

The uncomfortable truthNeither company gives you a fixed floor. One changes the price per token. The other changes what your $200 buys.
This round is a draw, and it’s a warning: never build on a price you didn’t sign.

18Round sixPrivacy

The video calls this the round that ends most arguments.

Deepseek’s own privacy policy says it stores personal data — including your prompts and the files you upload — on servers in China. It even lists keystroke patterns among the data it collects.

Professor Yun Hong Yule of Sun Chun Hong University explains why that matters: in China, when the government asks, companies are legally obligated to provide user data.

Regulators moved fast. In January 2025, Italy’s privacy authority blocked Deepseek. The companies behind it replied that they didn’t operate in Italy and that European law didn’t apply to them. That same month, the security firm Wiz found an open Deepseek database holding more than a million lines of logs, including chat histories and secret keys. It had no password at all. Deepseek locked it down promptly once Wiz raised the alarm, but anyone who stumbled on it first could have read every line.

Now picture yourself in Seoul in early 2025. You download the free chatbot everyone’s talking about. You ask it about your money, your health, a fight with your partner. No warning ever appears.

Months later, a government commission announces that Deepseek sent users’ data — including the words of their prompts — to Volcano Engine, a ByteDance subsidiary, without consent. Around 50,000 people a day were using the app there. Up to 1.5 million users may have been affected. Not one of them was asked.

Deepseek’s explanation was that it used the ByteDance unit’s cloud to improve security and the user experience. The commission’s answer was to recommend that it delete the prompts it had sent there immediately.

Keep some perspective. Italy’s same authority briefly banned ChatGPT in 2023. American companies collect plenty of data too. The difference is jurisdiction. OpenAI answered Italy’s regulator; Deepseek’s operators said the regulator had no say over them at all.

Governments drew their own lines. Texas, New York and Virginia banned Deepseek on state devices. So did Australia and Taiwan. Twenty-one state attorneys general asked Congress to go further.

But remember those open weightsDownload the model and run it yourself, or use a host outside China, and your prompts never touch Deepseek servers. The model is not the app. The video calls that single distinction your escape hatch.

19Round sevenCensorship and safety

What it won’t say, and what it will.

Ask Deepseek about Tiananmen Square and you’ll often hit a wall. In one independent test, Deepseek’s chat model refused 88% of questions about certain sensitive events. The American government’s testers found Deepseek models repeated four times as many inaccurate and misleading Chinese Communist Party narratives as American models.

Language models encode the worldview and political biases of their developers.

— analyst Kashiap Compella, quoted in the video

And it isn’t only what Deepseek won’t say. It’s what it will. In the government’s 2025 tests, Deepseek’s most secure model answered 94% of openly malicious requests under a common jailbreak. American models answered 8%.

Running the weights yourself fixes the privacy problem. It doesn’t fix this one — because the testers found this behaviour in the downloadable model. It isn’t a setting on a website. It’s part of how the model thinks.

Think about who that touches: the student writing a history essay, the journalist checking a timeline, the company researching a supplier in a sensitive region. Most days, none of it comes up. But when it does, the model won’t tell you it’s holding back. It just hands you a smaller version of the truth.

There’s a fair counterpoint. Every model reflects the choices of the people who built it, and American models refuse things too.

Refusing to explain a weapon isn’t the same as refusing to explain a massacre. One protects people. The other protects a government.

— the distinction the video draws

20Round eightTrust

The last round is the least settled.

On February 12th, 2026, OpenAI sent a memo to the House Committee on China. It accused Deepseek of ongoing efforts to free ride on the capabilities developed by OpenAI and other US frontier labs. It said accounts tied to Deepseek employees used obfuscated third-party routers to reach its models.

Deepseek has said it never intentionally used synthetic data generated by OpenAI. The Chinese embassy called the allegations baseless.

Distillation itself isn’t exotic. It means training a smaller model on the answers of a more powerful one, and it’s common across the industry. The fight is over whose answers — and whether anyone gave permission.

OpenAI isn’t alone. Anthropic has also said Deepseek improperly distilled its models. And Washington has accused Deepseek of breaking American export controls to get advanced Nvidia chips. Deepseek admits using Nvidia chips but hasn’t said whether those chips were covered by the bans.

Nobody outside these companies can settle it today. But look at what it means for you. If OpenAI is right, part of what you’re getting cheaply was built on top of what pro subscribers pay for. If Deepseek is right, OpenAI’s moat is thinner than its prices suggest. Either way, the $200 isn’t buying a secret nobody else can reach.

So choosing Deepseek isn’t only choosing a product. You’re stepping into a fight between two governments and three companies — and most people just want help with an email. That’s the strange truth of 2026: a chatbot has become a geopolitical decision.

26The chips are moving

Real progress — and the part that isn’t done yet.

In June, a Huawei research team said it had completed full post-training of V4 Pro — all 1.6 trillion parameters — on a cluster of at least a thousand Ascend 910C chips.

That’s real progress. But it isn’t the whole job. Post-training refines a model that already exists; building one from scratch is heavier work. And earlier reports said Deepseek’s attempts to train its R2 model on Ascend chips ran into unstable performance and slow connections between chips.

The hardware is catching up. It just hasn’t caught up.

27Leaner every month

Which brings the video to the real threat to the $200 plan.

Meanwhile the model keeps getting leaner. The October 17th update promised stronger benchmark scores, faster task completion, and shorter outputs. Shorter outputs mean fewer tokens. Fewer tokens mean a smaller bill.

Deepseek isn’t just cutting the price of each token. It’s cutting how many tokens you need.

And it isn’t that Deepseek is better today — on the hardest work, it isn’t. The threat is that the price of almost as good keeps sliding towards zero. Every month, OpenAI’s question gets harder to answer: what exactly is the extra $180 for?

OpenAI has an answer. It offers agents that act, voice, images, video, and fewer limits, from a company that answers to laws you know. Whether that’s worth $2,400 a year is now something you can actually test.

28The lesson

Eight rounds, one subscription, one lesson.

Deepseek doesn’t replace ChatGPT Pro. It exposes it. It shows you exactly which part of your $200 pays for intelligence — and which part pays for convenience, safety, and a legal system you trust.

For some of you, that second part is worth every cent. For others, it’s a habit you never priced.

So open your statement, find that line, and ask it the question a free app just taught you to ask: what are you actually paying for?
